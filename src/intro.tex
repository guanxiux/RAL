Dense convolution dominates per-frame latency in vision tasks where reaction
time decides the outcome~\cite{nanoflownet,fastdepth,perclatency}, such as a
car avoiding a pedestrian stepping out of occlusion~\cite{avarch}, a drone
dodging an obstacle~\cite{eventavoid}, or a robot returning a table-tennis
ball~\cite{robottabletennis} (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). Yet on a given input
much of that computation is spatially redundant. A
common remedy is masked convolution: an upstream policy marks spatially
active positions on the dense feature grid, and a dedicated sparse
convolution operator updates only these positions in the dense output. The mask may derive
from event-camera increments~\cite{evconv,asyncsparse}, temporal
differences~\cite{deltacnn,skipconv}, or learned spatial
gates~\cite{dynconv,dgnet}. These mask sources promise large reductions in
convolution work, yet existing masked-convolution
systems~\cite{evconv,deltacnn,dgnet,dynconv} rarely
translate the sparsity into proportional latency reduction.

Dense GPU convolution obtains high \emph{throughput} (operations per second)
from coordinate-driven, tiled execution. Each tile enumerates a contiguous
block of output coordinates; those coordinates fix each output position's
receptive field and write location, while the reduction over channels and
kernel offsets follows a regular datapath with coalesced loads and
weight-tile reuse.
In masked convolution, reducing latency proportionally requires
sustaining this throughput while achieving a high \emph{useful-compute ratio}
(the fraction of issued operations that produce needed outputs), but the
per-frame position selection imposed by the mask conflicts with the contiguous
coordinate assumption that sustains throughput, placing these two
axes in tension.

Existing systems sacrifice one axis for the other.
\emph{Gather-scatter}~\cite{sai,dgnet,dynconv} extracts exactly the
active positions into a packed representation, computes, and
scatters the results back, but is bottlenecked by the memory traffic of
materializing their receptive fields.
\emph{Tile skipping}~\cite{sbnet,skipconv,evconv,deltacnn} suppresses tiles that are entirely inactive and retains the dense
pipeline, but wastes computation on partially active tiles.
On an RTX~3080 running YOLOv8m~\cite{yolov8} at batch~4 under temporal
difference masks~\cite{deltacnn}, 82.2\% of convolution work is unnecessary.
Gather-scatter sustains only 43.7\% of the dense pipeline's throughput,
reducing latency by just 59.3\%; tile skipping reaches higher throughput but
wastes 32.6\% of issued operations, reducing latency by 54.3\%, slower
than gather-scatter despite its throughput advantage
(\S\ref{sec:bg:gap}).

We find that redirecting a dense tiled pipeline to compute only scattered
active positions achieves both a high useful-compute ratio and the throughput
of dense execution. We collect these
positions into a worklist and characterize (\S\ref{sec:char}) the resulting tiled execution on a
desktop and an embedded GPU, identifying why: because the input stays a dense
grid, the irregularity reduces to one index per output row, outside the
reduction loop that drives coalesced channel loads and weight-tile reuse, so
per-tile throughput matches dense execution. When the tile configuration follows the
worklist length, convolution time is affine in the active ratio with a slope matching
the dense marginal cost; the residual gap is an intercept of 5.6--17.4\% of
dense time per layer (median per GPU and precision), dominated by kernel launch latency and fixed per-tile stalls such as barriers,
not by throughput loss.

While this finding establishes that worklist-driven tiled execution can
sustain both axes, closing the gap end to end requires solving two further
challenges.
First, \emph{worklist construction cost}: deriving the worklist from the
mask requires scanning the whole grid and costs time independent of the active count, so construction's share of
latency grows as activity falls and accumulates when every layer rebuilds.
Second, \emph{worklist consumption efficiency}: the affine property holds for
a given number of active positions under a matching tile configuration, but
in deployment this number is visible only on the device after construction and
shifts across frames and layers. Reading it back to the host stalls the
per-frame path; adapting on the device avoids the stall but incurs a
non-negligible cost per decision point (\S\ref{sec:char}).

Based on these findings, we present \sysname
(\textbf{W}orklist-dr\textbf{I}ven ma\textbf{S}k\textbf{E}d
\textbf{Conv}o\-lu\-tion), which drives the dense tiled pipeline with an
active-output worklist to achieve both a high useful-compute ratio and high
throughput by co-designing worklist construction and consumption.
To reduce construction cost, a single mask-space pass fuses output-mask
propagation with per-tile compaction, operating on only mask and coordinate
metadata; compatible operators reuse the worklist instead of
reconstructing it.
To sustain consumption efficiency, a persistent compute grid consumes the
worklist without host synchronization, and a cost-aware selection mechanism
adapts the tile configuration per frame, adding a candidate to a layer only
where its expected gain exceeds the selection overhead.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We characterize worklist-driven tiled execution, 
        identifying why computing scattered active positions preserves
        dense-level throughput and where the remaining gap comes from.
        These findings motivate and constrain the design
        (\S\ref{sec:char}).

  \item We present \sysname, which co-designs worklist construction,
        worklist-driven tiled compute, and cost-aware tile selection to
        jointly sustain high useful-compute ratio and high throughput
        (\S\ref{sec:design}).

  % geomean over 44 workload-device-precision cases (fastest available baseline, dense included), 2026-11-10
  \item We evaluate \sysname on four workloads across six GPUs. \sysname
        achieves a 2.18$\times$ (FP32) and 2.22$\times$ (FP16)
        geometric-mean speedup over the fastest baseline per
        pair, and reduces energy by 60.8--90.1\% relative to dense
        execution on embedded GPUs (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}
