\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

We ask three questions: whether \sysname turns spatial sparsity into
end-to-end latency and energy reduction across GPU classes
(\S\ref{sec:eval:end-to-end}), where its remaining time goes
(\S\ref{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}), and whether each co-designed component is
necessary (\S\ref{sec:eval:ablation-sensitivity}).

\paragraph{Platforms.}
We evaluate six NVIDIA GPUs that span datacenter, desktop, laptop, and
embedded classes: A100, RTX~4090, RTX~3080, RTX~4070 Laptop, Jetson AGX Orin,
and Jetson Xavier NX. GPU clocks are locked on every platform so that
latencies are stable across runs. On the two Jetson boards, where the energy
budget constrains deployment, we also report per-frame energy from the board's
input power.

\paragraph{Workloads.}
Three models (Table~\ref{tab:workloads}) cover the three mask sources of
\S\ref{sec:bg:masked}: event masks, temporal-difference masks, and learned
spatial gates. YOLOv8m runs at batch~1 (b1: a single latency-bound camera, our
primary setting) and batch~4 (b4: four concurrent camera streams), giving four
workloads. Each mask policy follows its upstream method; where it needs
thresholds, they are tuned on data disjoint from the evaluation set.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
\emph{Dense} runs the original model with PyTorch and cuDNN~\cite{cudnn}
autotuning: it computes every position and carries none of the masking
machinery (no mask generation, propagation, or temporal state).
\emph{Tile skipping} uses DeltaCNN's kernels~\cite{deltacnn}, and
\emph{gather-scatter} uses MMCV's masked convolution~\cite{mmcv} (DynConv's
own CUDA backend~\cite{dynconv} for pose). All paths run the same weights and
inputs, and the three masked paths the same layer masks, so they differ only
in the masked-convolution backend. Every path except gather-scatter replays as
a CUDA graph; gather-scatter reads each layer's active count on the host to
size its buffers and its GEMM, so it must run eagerly. Every path runs in both
precisions except gather-scatter on DynConv in FP16: DynConv's kernels support
only FP32, and MMCV's masked convolution does not support the grouped
convolutions of its network. We report \sysname's speedup over the
fastest baseline on each workload and platform.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Latency is the GPU time of one frame (of one batch of four for YOLOv8m b4)
measured with CUDA events; it includes worklist construction and all
non-convolution operators. YOLOv8m processes whole videos continuously, so its
temporal masks evolve as in deployment.
\sysname's per-device tuning (\S\ref{sec:design:select}) runs once offline
and is excluded. Task accuracy is reported to confirm that the sparse paths
agree.

\label{sec:eval:end-to-end}

\paragraph{Latency.}
\sysname is the fastest path in all 44 groups of workload, platform, and
precision (Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}). In FP32 it runs 1.49--3.23$\times$
faster than the fastest baseline of each group (geometric mean 2.06$\times$),
and in FP16 1.27--4.25$\times$ faster (2.09$\times$), 2.07$\times$ over all
44 groups. \S\ref{sec:eval:microbenchmarks} traces this advantage to worklist-driven
tiled execution, whose tiles restrict computation to active positions at the
per-tile cost of dense tiles (Insight~2). No baseline is consistently second in FP32: tile skipping is the
fastest baseline in 13 of the 24 groups, gather-scatter in 6, and Dense in 5.

In FP16, Dense is the fastest baseline in 19 of the 20 groups, because tensor
cores shorten its convolution while DeltaCNN's FP16 kernels run on CUDA cores
and gather-scatter's host synchronization does not depend on precision.
Gather-scatter varies most, because its per-layer extraction, scatter, and host
synchronization do not shrink with the active ratio. In 23 groups, mostly
FireFlowNet, DynConv, and YOLOv8m b1, it runs 1.85--12.4$\times$ slower than
Dense and extends beyond the axis. On YOLOv8m b4, four streams amortize these
costs and make it the fastest baseline on four of the six GPUs in FP32, where
\sysname still runs 1.69--2.39$\times$ faster.

The distance to the ideal line follows worklist length, as Insight~4
predicts. As a geometric mean over GPUs and precisions, \sysname runs at
1.64$\times$ the ideal latency on YOLOv8m b4, whose median worklist holds 1{,}382 entries, and
at 2.78$\times$ on DynConv, whose median worklist holds 43
(Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency}), so per-layer fixed costs dominate
DynConv's frame.

\paragraph{Energy.}
Shorter frames translate into lower energy (Table~\ref{tab:e2e-energy}).
\sysname is the lowest-energy path for every workload on both Jetson boards:
it uses 32.8--51.8\% less energy per frame than the lowest-energy baseline in
FP32 and 60.8--79.9\% less in FP16, and across both precisions 60.8--82.5\%
less than Dense.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
Given the same layer masks, the three masked paths reach the same accuracy
(Table~\ref{tab:e2e-accuracy}): AEE is identical, PCKh differs by at most
0.01 points, and YOLOv8m AP by at most 0.3 points, residuals of
floating-point reduction order. The remaining gap to Dense (1.4 PCKh points lower on DynConv, slightly better
AEE and AP elsewhere) is set by the upstream mask policy.

\label{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}

\paragraph{Effective throughput.}
Figure~\ref{fig:effective-throughput} separates the end-to-end result into
the two axes of Eq.~\ref{eq:efficiency-framework-throughput}, useful-compute
ratio $\eta$ and raw throughput $T$; the two baselines fail on opposite
axes. Tile skipping
keeps the dense tile schedule, reaching 17.5--59.9\% of Dense raw throughput in
FP32 (7.9--19.7\% in FP16, where its kernels run on CUDA cores and Dense's on
tensor cores), but computes every position of an active tile, so $\eta$ is
0.49--0.80. Gather-scatter issues only required positions ($\eta = 1$), but
extraction and writeback hold its raw throughput to 2.6--42.0\% of Dense
(4.4--15.4\% in FP16). \sysname keeps $\eta$ at 0.91--1.00 and sustains
52.3--76.0\% of Dense raw throughput in FP32 (29.6--73.2\% in FP16), the
highest effective throughput in every group.
Its tiles run at the per-tile cost of dense tiles (Insight~2), so the raw
throughput it gives up is the per-layer fixed cost of construction and launch
(Insight~4), which weighs most where layers are short, as in FP16.

\input{figures/worklist_efficiency_table}

Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency} locates \sysname's remaining loss with the
decomposition of Eq.~\ref{eq:eta-decomp}. Tile-local compaction emits each
active coordinate exactly once ($\eta_{\mathrm{construct}} = 100\%$), so all
loss is padding in the final tile of each worklist ($\eta_{\mathrm{compute}}$)
and it follows worklist length: 99.8\% and 98.1\% on FireFlowNet and YOLOv8m
b4, whose median worklists hold thousands of entries, and 93.6\% and 92.1\% on
YOLOv8m b1 and DynConv, whose shortest worklists (P10) hold 26 and 3 entries.

\paragraph{Latency decomposition.}
The latency difference comes from what each path does outside convolution
(Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition}). We split the busy part of each path's frame by
its kernels' shares of GPU busy time; host sync is the rest of the frame, when the GPU waits
for the host, and gather-scatter's gather and scatter include compacting the
mask into the index list it gathers by.
At b1 in FP32, \sysname cuts convolution from 7.21 to 2.27\,ms at the cost of
0.17\,ms of construction, and keeps the GPU busy because the compute path never
returns to the host.

Gather-scatter spends the least time in convolution (1.68\,ms) because it
multiplies compact buffers, but filling and draining them takes another
1.95\,ms of gather and scatter: moving each receptive field through memory
costs more than \sysname's tiles reading it in place from the dense input
(3.63\,ms against 2.44\,ms for \sysname's construction and convolution).
The buffers also tie every layer to the host: sizing them and the GEMM needs
the layer's active count, which exists only on the device, so each layer reads
it back before it launches. These round trips leave the GPU idle for 8.26\,ms,
64.8\% of its frame, and even its busy time alone (4.48\,ms) is 1.49$\times$
\sysname's 3.00\,ms.

At b4, four streams hide most of that idle time and bring it to
4.50\,ms per frame, still 2.39$\times$ \sysname's 1.88\,ms. In FP16, tensor
cores shrink convolution more than the rest of the frame: construction and
other operators grow from 24.3\% to 39.0\% of \sysname's b1 frame, and its
speedup over Dense narrows from 2.79$\times$ to 2.53$\times$.

\paragraph{Speedup across activity.}
Grouping the frames of YOLOv8m b1 on RTX~3080 by their active ratio tests the
affine behavior end to end. \sysname's speedup over Dense falls smoothly with
activity, from 4.59$\times$ for frames with $\alpha<0.02$ to 1.31$\times$ for
$\alpha\ge0.4$ in FP32 (3.14$\times$ to 1.43$\times$ in FP16), as the affine
model with a per-layer intercept predicts (Insights~1 and~4). Neither baseline
turns low activity into a speedup: both run slower than Dense at every active
ratio. The selection of \S\ref{sec:design:select} places
its gains where the uniform prior expects them: against the single
configuration with the best expected speedup (speedup-optimal,
\S\ref{sec:eval:ablation-sensitivity}), \sysname is 1.13$\times$ faster on the sparsest frames and at
most 4\% slower on the densest (FP32).

\paragraph{Memory overhead.}
\sysname's metadata adds little memory (Table~\ref{tab:memory}). The temporal
mask policies of FireFlowNet and YOLOv8m retain previous-frame activations, so
every masked path allocates 3.8--6.3$\times$ Dense, and \sysname stays within
the range of the other masked paths (128.6 vs.\ 141.8--197.0\,MiB on
FireFlowNet). DynConv's learned gates keep no temporal state, and \sysname
allocates 0.1\,MiB more than Dense.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:eval:ablation-sensitivity}

\input{figures/ablation_table}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} replaces one mechanism at a time on YOLOv8m (b1,
FP16) on RTX~3080 and AGX Orin. Rebuilding the worklist at every layer (No
reuse) nearly doubles construction time (0.16 to 0.30\,ms and 0.48 to
0.84\,ms) and adds 9.9\% and 6.2\% to frame latency. A shuffled worklist
lengthens convolution by 4.7\% and 4.4\% and the frame by 2.8\% and 2.6\%, as
Insight~3 predicts (\S\ref{sec:char:order}). Reading every row's input from a
kernel map at every tap (Kernel map), as kernel-map implicit GEMM does, raises
construction to 0.25 and 0.73\,ms with the map build and convolution to 0.96
and 3.82\,ms with the map lookups, so the frame runs 1.12$\times$ and
1.14$\times$ slower (\S\ref{sec:char:reduction}). Launching the dense layer's grid,
whose CTAs beyond the worklist exit, lengthens convolution by 19.4\% and
13.1\%, the cost that the persistent grid avoids (\S\ref{sec:design:compute}).
Reading $n_W$ back to the host at every layer to launch the exact grid runs
6.51$\times$ and 5.08$\times$ slower, as Insight~5 predicts.

The tile-configuration variants isolate what following $n_W$ gains. Fixing
every layer to the configuration fastest at $\alpha = 1$ runs 1.71$\times$ and
1.28$\times$ slower; the latency-optimal configuration of
\S\ref{sec:char:affine} runs 1.10$\times$ slower on both GPUs.
The speedup-optimal configuration comes within
1.6\% of \sysname on RTX~3080 but runs 1.09$\times$ slower on AGX Orin, where
its convolution takes 3.83 instead of 3.34\,ms. The two GPUs differ in how
many layers follow the length: the selection (\S\ref{sec:design:select}) adds
candidates to a layer only where the gain exceeds that GPU's per-layer
selection cost, which holds for 5 of 83 layers on RTX~3080 and for 24 on AGX
Orin, whose per-layer gains are larger (Insight~5). Placing four candidates at
every layer regardless of this cost (Cost-unaware $K_{\max}$) shortens
convolution by 1.7\% and 5.4\%, but every producer then takes the selecting
path and construction grows from 0.16 to 0.22\,ms and from 0.48 to 0.65\,ms,
so the frame runs 1.07$\times$ and 1.02$\times$ slower, most on RTX~3080,
where the gains that the cost model declines are smallest.

The zero-cost oracle runs the same candidates as Cost-unaware $K_{\max}$ on a
graph captured for each frame's own $n_W$, with no selection mechanism (only
the replay is timed), and runs at 0.92$\times$ and 0.88$\times$ \sysname's
latency. Both pick the same tile for every layer and frame, so the gap between
them (0.85 vs.\ 0.75\,ms and 3.16 vs.\ 2.79\,ms of convolution, 0.22 vs.\
0.15\,ms and 0.65 vs.\ 0.41\,ms of construction) is the cost of the
device-side mechanism itself. The device-updatable node that \sysname uses is
the cheapest mechanism measured in \S\ref{sec:char:cost}, so this margin is
what following $n_W$ costs on current GPUs; a launch predicate evaluated
before CTA allocation would remove it (\S\ref{sec:eval:discussion}).

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\sysname converts spatial sparsity into latency, so its benefit is bounded by
how sparse the upstream masks are and by how much of the frame convolution
occupies. First, the gain vanishes as the active ratio approaches one: at full
activity the per-length best configuration only matches dense execution
(\S\ref{sec:char:affine}) while construction still runs, so a fully active
input runs slightly slower than the dense path. \sysname consumes masks and
makes no claim about their quality or the resulting accuracy; a policy that
keeps most positions active should stay on the dense path. Second, \sysname
accelerates convolution only, so convolution's share of frame latency caps the
end-to-end speedup. FP16 tensor cores shrink that share, and the remaining
non-convolution operators become the next bottleneck, which is why FP16
speedups are narrower than FP32 ones (\S\ref{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}).
Models dominated by other operators bound the gain further.

\paragraph{Hardware implications.}
The characterization traces the remaining gap to two per-layer costs that
current GPUs leave to software. The intercept of the affine model is launch
and dependency latency rather than lost bandwidth (Insight~4), and its share
grows as inputs become sparser; hardware that shortens the path from launch to
the first useful instruction (for example, by staging kernel parameters before
the CTA starts) and the dependent chain of a single tile would move sparse
execution toward the proportional line. Following the worklist length costs
about as much per decision point as it gains (Insight~5), because every
existing mechanism either returns to the host or reconfigures the graph. A
launch predicate that the hardware evaluates on a device-resident value before
allocating CTA resources, or a work distributor that picks the tile shape from
the worklist length, would let every layer adapt its configuration at
negligible cost.
