\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

We ask three questions: whether \sysname turns spatial sparsity into
end-to-end latency and energy reduction across GPU classes
(\S\ref{sec:eval:end-to-end}), where its remaining time goes
(\S\ref{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}), and whether each co-designed component is
necessary (\S\ref{sec:eval:ablation-sensitivity}).

\paragraph{Platforms.}
We evaluate six NVIDIA GPUs that span datacenter, desktop, laptop, and
embedded classes: A100, RTX~4090, RTX~3080, RTX~4070 Laptop, Jetson AGX Orin,
and Jetson Xavier NX. GPU clocks are locked on every platform so that
latencies are stable across runs. On the two Jetson boards, where the energy
budget constrains deployment, we also report per-frame energy from the board's
input power.

\paragraph{Workloads.}
The three workloads (Table~\ref{tab:workloads}) cover the three mask sources
of \S\ref{sec:bg:masked}: event masks, temporal-difference masks, and learned
spatial gates. Each mask policy follows its upstream method; where it needs
thresholds, they are tuned on data disjoint from the evaluation set.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
\emph{Dense} runs the original model with PyTorch and cuDNN~\cite{cudnn}
autotuning: it computes every position and carries none of the masking
machinery (no mask generation, propagation, or temporal state).
\emph{Tile skipping} uses DeltaCNN's kernels~\cite{deltacnn}, and
\emph{gather-scatter} uses MMCV's masked convolution~\cite{mmcv} (DynConv's
own CUDA backend~\cite{dynconv} for pose). All paths run the same weights and
inputs, and the three masked paths the same layer masks, so they differ only
in the masked-convolution backend. Every path except gather-scatter replays as
a CUDA graph; gather-scatter reads each layer's active count on the host to
size its buffers, so it must run eagerly. The baselines implement only FP32, so
FP16 compares \sysname with Dense alone. We report \sysname's speedup over the
fastest baseline on each workload and platform.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Latency is the GPU time of one frame measured with CUDA events; it includes
worklist construction and all non-convolution operators. YOLOv8m processes whole videos continuously, so its
temporal masks evolve as in deployment, at batch~1 (YOLOv8m b1: a single
latency-bound camera, our primary setting) and batch~4 (YOLOv8m b4: four
concurrent camera streams).
\sysname's per-device tuning (\S\ref{sec:design:select}) runs once offline
and is excluded. Task accuracy is reported to confirm that the sparse paths
agree.

\label{sec:eval:end-to-end}

\paragraph{Latency.}
\sysname is the fastest path in all 24
measured workload-platform pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}). Relative to
the fastest evaluated competing path in each pair, it achieves a 1.58$\times$
geometric-mean speedup; individual-pair speedups range from 1.33$\times$ to
2.12$\times$, corresponding to 25.0--52.8\% lower latency. Gains span more
than two orders of magnitude in absolute latency, from 0.23\,ms for
FireFlowNet on RTX~4090 to 88.6\,ms for YOLOv8m on Xavier NX.

The two existing sparse paths split the remaining 24 pairs between them: dense
is fastest in 13 and tile skipping in 11; gather-scatter never wins. The split
tracks active ratio: at the YOLO workloads' $\alpha\approx48$--$49\%$, few
tiles are fully inactive, so tile skipping cannot compensate for its lower
throughput; on FireFlowNet ($\alpha\approx25\%$) and DynConv
($\alpha\approx16\%$), enough tiles are skippable for it to overtake dense.
Neither path is uniformly strong, and \sysname outperforms whichever wins.
On the Jetson platforms the lead narrows
(geometric mean 1.53$\times$ vs.\ 1.61$\times$ on discrete GPUs) but \sysname
remains fastest in all eight pairs.

Across all 24 pairs, \sysname averages 1.76$\times$ the ideal
proportional-scaling reference (dashed lines in
Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}), compared with 2.79$\times$ for the per-pair
fastest competing path. The gap to ideal tracks active ratio: on the YOLO
workloads \sysname runs at 1.36$\times$ ideal, while on DynConv
($\alpha\approx16\%$) construction and fixed costs widen it to
2.60$\times$.

\paragraph{Energy.}
\sysname is the lowest-energy path for every workload on both Jetson
platforms (Table~\ref{tab:e2e-energy}). In FP32 it uses 32.8--51.8\% less
energy per frame than the lowest-energy baseline, and in FP16 60.8--83.6\% less
than Dense.

\input{figures/e2e_energy_table}

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
\sysname's latency and energy gains do not change accuracy
(Table~\ref{tab:e2e-accuracy}). Given the same layer masks, the three masked
paths agree: AEE and PCKh are identical, and YOLOv8m AP differs by at most 0.1
points, a residual of floating-point reduction order rather than of covered
positions. Where worklist reuse computes a superset of the required positions,
the output mask gates writeback, so the extra positions never reach downstream
layers. The remaining gap to Dense (1.4 PCKh points lower on DynConv, slightly
better AEE and AP on the other two) is set by the upstream mask policy, which
\sysname consumes without modification.

\paragraph{Effective throughput.}
A masked path lowers latency only if it is both selective, captured by the
useful-compute ratio $\eta$, and fast, captured by raw throughput $T$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:efficiency-framework-throughput}).
Figure~\ref{fig:effective-throughput} reports both, and the two baselines fail
on opposite axes. Tile skipping keeps the dense tile schedule but computes
every position of an active tile, so its $\eta$ is only 0.49--0.74 while its
raw throughput reaches 19.0--77.5\% of Dense. Gather-scatter issues exactly the
required positions ($\eta=1$), but extraction and writeback hold its raw
throughput to 2.6--66.5\% of Dense. \sysname keeps $\eta$ at 0.91--1.00 and
sustains 50.8--120.2\% of Dense raw throughput in FP32 (29.6--83.6\% in FP16),
the highest of the masked paths in every case. On YOLOv8m b4 its effective
throughput exceeds even Dense's raw throughput, because cuDNN runs this
configuration slower than \sysname's kernels.

\input{figures/worklist_efficiency_table}

Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency} localizes \sysname's own loss through the
decomposition of Eq.~\ref{eq:eta-decomp}. $\eta_{\mathrm{construct}}$ is
100.0\% on every workload: tile-local compaction emits each active coordinate
exactly once. All of the loss is in $\eta_{\mathrm{compute}}$, the fraction of
issued slots that are worklist entries, and it tracks worklist length.
FireFlowNet and YOLOv8m b4, whose median worklists hold thousands of entries,
reach 99.8\% and 98.1\%; YOLOv8m b1 and DynConv, whose worklists are often
shorter than a few tiles (P10 of 24 and 3 entries), lose 7.6\% and 7.9\% to the
padding of their final tiles.

\paragraph{Latency decomposition.}
Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition} splits YOLOv8m latency by stage. At batch~1 in
FP32, \sysname cuts convolution from 9.68 to 2.27\,ms (4.3$\times$) at the cost
of 0.17\,ms of construction, and the frame falls from 10.76 to 3.00\,ms.
Gather-scatter issues the least convolution of any masked path (1.68\,ms), but
its per-layer host synchronization leaves the GPU idle for 8.26\,ms, 64.8\% of
its frame. Batching four streams hides most of that idle time (0.87\,ms per
frame) and lets both baselines fill the GPU, so tile skipping and
gather-scatter fall to 5.04 and 4.50\,ms per frame; \sysname still leads at
1.88\,ms because its batch-1 grid already keeps the GPU busy. In FP16, tensor
cores shrink convolution more than the rest of the frame: construction and
other operators grow from 24.3\% of \sysname's FP32 frame to 39.6\%, and the
speedup over Dense narrows from 3.59$\times$ to 2.63$\times$.

\emph{Construction.} On RTX~3080, Atomic append and Global order raise construction from
0.12 to 0.43 and 0.54\,ms without reducing convolution (0.94\,ms throughout),
adding 19.1\% and 19.6\% to the end-to-end latency over Ours; Global order's
higher construction reflects its additional kernel passes. No reuse
\emph{raises} $\eta$ to 99.7\% ($\eta_{\mathrm{construct}}=1$ per layer) but
increases construction 1.6$\times$ (0.12 to 0.19\,ms), adding 5.7\% over Ours:
rebuilding the worklist every layer is more expensive than reusing a slightly loose
superset worklist. On AGX~Orin the same pattern holds with smaller margins:
construction variants add 1--9\% over Ours while convolution stays within
0.05\,ms, because convolution is a larger share of the total.

\emph{Compute.} On RTX~3080, DP only raises convolution from 0.94 to 1.01\,ms over Ours
(+0.07\,ms); No hybrid, which retains full Stream-$K$ but not the hybrid
mode, recovers most of that gap (0.96\,ms, +0.02\,ms over Ours). Readback \&
launch runs the same kernels as Ours but adds per-operator host
synchronization, inflating the total from 1.66 to 7.61\,ms (4.58$\times$
Ours). On AGX~Orin, DP only adds 1.5\% end-to-end latency over Ours and
readback inflates it to 2.38$\times$ Ours.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\sysname converts spatial sparsity into latency, so its benefit is bounded by
how sparse the upstream masks are and by how much of the frame convolution
occupies. First, the gain vanishes as the active ratio approaches one: at full
activity the per-length best configuration only matches dense execution
(\S\ref{sec:char:affine}) while construction still runs, so a fully active
input runs slightly slower than the dense path. \sysname consumes masks and
makes no claim about their quality or the resulting accuracy; a policy that
keeps most positions active should stay on the dense path. Second, \sysname
accelerates convolution only, so convolution's share of frame latency caps the
end-to-end speedup. FP16 tensor cores shrink that share, and the remaining
non-convolution operators become the next bottleneck, which is why FP16
speedups are narrower than FP32 ones (\S\ref{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}).
Models dominated by other operators bound the gain further.

\paragraph{Hardware implications.}
The characterization traces the remaining gap to two per-layer costs that
current GPUs leave to software. The intercept of the affine model is launch
and dependency latency rather than lost bandwidth (Insight~4), and its share
grows as inputs become sparser; hardware that shortens the path from launch to
the first useful instruction (for example, by staging kernel parameters before
the CTA starts) and the dependent chain of a single tile would move sparse
execution toward the proportional line. Following the worklist length costs
about as much per decision point as it gains (Insight~5), because every
existing mechanism either returns to the host or reconfigures the graph. A
launch predicate that the hardware evaluates on a device-resident value before
allocating CTA resources, or a work distributor that picks the tile shape from
the worklist length, would let every layer adapt its configuration at
negligible cost.
