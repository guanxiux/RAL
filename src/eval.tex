\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

\paragraph{Platforms.}

We evaluate six GPU platforms spanning four discrete GPUs and two embedded
SoCs. The discrete platforms are RTX~4070 Laptop, RTX~3080,
\textit{TBD Discrete GPU A}, and \textit{TBD Discrete GPU B}.
The four discrete GPUs cover a range of compute capabilities. Jetson Xavier NX
and Jetson AGX Orin extend the evaluation to embedded SoCs with limited compute
and power budgets, where we also measure board energy.

\paragraph{Models, tasks, and mask sources.}
Our workloads span three vision tasks.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Event-based optical flow.}
        FireFlowNet~\cite{fireflownet} is a lightweight event-based optical-flow
        model that delivers high inference rates while retaining competitive
        accuracy. We evaluate it on MVSEC~\cite{mvsec}, which contains
        event-camera sequences and corresponding ground-truth flow from indoor
        quadrotor flight and outdoor driving. Following Ev-Conv~\cite{evconv},
        consecutive 50-ms windows advance by 1~ms; events entering or leaving
        the window form the sparse increment from which we derive layer masks.

  \item \textbf{Detection.} We evaluate YOLOv8n and
        YOLOv8m~\cite{yolov8} on MOT16~\cite{mot16}, a collection of crowded
        pedestrian videos for multi-object tracking. Their two model sizes
        expose how scale affects sparse-execution efficiency. For both models,
        DeltaCNN's temporal policy~\cite{deltacnn} thresholds activation
        differences between successive frames and propagates the resulting
        masks across layers.

  \item \textbf{Human pose estimation.} We use the DynConv pose
        model~\cite{dynconv} on MPII~\cite{mpii}, which depicts people
        performing diverse activities and annotates their 2D body joints.
        DynConv's learned spatial gates produce an input-dependent mask for
        each gated block.
\end{itemize}


Table~\ref{tab:models} summarizes the workloads and evaluation scales.
For every workload, calibration uses the training split, while all reported
results use a disjoint test split. We use
the authors' released weights for all four models~\cite{fireflownet,yolov8,dynconv}.
Within each workload, we fix the inputs, weights, and upstream mask policy;
every sparse path receives the same resulting layer masks, isolating the
masked-convolution backend.

\paragraph{Baselines.}

We compare \sysname against three FP32 execution paths established by prior
work:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dense} executes the complete model with the native
        PyTorch/cuDNN backend~\cite{cudnn}.

  \item \textbf{Tile skipping} adapts DeltaCNN's CUDA
        implementation~\cite{deltacnn}, retaining the dense tiled pipeline with a fused selective
        path and
        skipping a tile only when all its outputs are inactive.

  \item \textbf{Gather-scatter} uses DynConv's original CUDA backend for
        pose~\cite{dynconv}. FireFlowNet and YOLO use an adaptation of MMCV's
        masked-convolution operator~\cite{mmcv}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Measurements.}

We measure per-frame full-model GPU latency over the complete evaluation data
above with CUDA events; every sequence or split is repeated three times. Timing
covers mask propagation, worklist construction, all network operators, and
output update; it excludes input transfer, offline calibration and autotuning,
and one-time state initialization. Since we focus on end-to-end performance,
we report $\eta$, $T$, and $T_{\mathrm{eff}}$ at the model level by
accumulating required and issued convolution work across all layers within each
frame before applying Eq.~\ref{eq:efficiency-framework}, then averaging the
frame-level values across frames. On the Jetson platforms, we integrate
board-level \texttt{VDD\_IN} over each timed round to obtain per-frame board energy. We follow the original workload
protocols: AEE for optical flow~\cite{evconv}, COCO-style average precision
(AP) for detection, averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to
0.95~\cite{deltacnn}, and PCKh for human pose~\cite{dynconv}. Because
MOT16 releases ground-truth detections only for its training split, AP on the
test sequences uses fixed pseudo-labels from a dense YOLO26x
teacher~\cite{yolo26}.

\paragraph{Latency.}
\sysname is the fastest path in all 16 measured workload-platform pairs
(Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}). Relative to the fastest competing path for each
pair, it reduces full-model latency by 34.2--46.5\% on the discrete GPUs and
22.1--38.6\% on the Jetson SoCs. These gains persist across all four workloads
and both device classes despite a more than two-order-of-magnitude span in
absolute latency, from 0.399\,ms for FireFlowNet on RTX~3080 to 94.3\,ms for
YOLOv8m on Xavier NX.

Existing sparse paths realize the available headroom only in narrow regimes:
tile skipping needs low activity to offset tile-level waste, while
gather-scatter needs sufficiently heavy convolution to amortize its overhead.
FireFlowNet and DynConv leave enough removable work for tile skipping to
cut dense latency by up to 42.0\% and 40.9\%. The higher-activity
DeltaCNN masks for YOLOv8n and YOLOv8m leave less headroom, and tile skipping
fails to turn it into a material speedup: it is near parity with dense on AGX
Orin and slower in the other six pairs.
Gather-scatter becomes
the fastest existing path only for YOLOv8m on AGX Orin, where the heavier
convolution makes the arithmetic saved by exact position selection outweigh
gather-scatter's extraction and writeback overhead. Despite these
regime-dependent baseline gains, \sysname remains fastest in every pair.

Across all workloads, \sysname is substantially closer to the ideal reference
than the fastest existing path. For FireFlowNet and the two YOLO models,
\sysname runs at
1.19--2.10$\times$ the reference, while the fastest existing path remains at
1.88--3.21$\times$. As the active ratio falls, active convolution work shrinks
while mask processing, worklist construction, and other fixed model work do
not, magnifying these costs relative to the ideal reference. DynConv
makes this effect most visible: it has the lowest active ratio and additionally
runs learned mask-prediction kernels in every gated block. Even so, \sysname runs at
2.34--3.66$\times$ the reference, while the fastest existing path remains at
3.80--6.43$\times$.

\paragraph{Energy.}
\sysname's latency gains also reduce per-frame board energy on both Jetson
platforms. It is the lowest-energy path for every workload
(Table~\ref{tab:e2e-energy}), reducing energy by
17.0--39.8\% relative to the lowest-energy competing path in each pair and by
28.5--72.6\% relative to dense execution.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
\sysname's latency and energy gains preserve the accuracy of the selected
sparsity policies. For FireFlowNet and DynConv, its AEE and PCKh match the
other sparse paths at the reported precision. In the
YOLOv8 models, small floating-point differences accumulate across frames under
temporal execution; even so, \sysname's AP differs from the other sparse paths
by at most 0.5\% in relative terms (Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-accuracy}). Compared
with dense execution, the upstream sparsity policies lower AP and PCKh by only
1.6--3.5\%, while lowering FireFlowNet's AEE by 0.8\%.

\paragraph{Effective throughput.}
Under the same execution settings as the end-to-end evaluation, we estimate
full-model throughput $T$ and effective throughput $\eta T$ on RTX~3080
(Figure~\ref{fig:effective-throughput}). Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency}
reports \sysname's $\eta$ decomposition and worklist-length distribution.
\sysname sustains 35.3--89.7\% of dense throughput, the highest among sparse
paths in every workload at 1.10--1.99$\times$ that of the next-highest sparse
path.
DeltaCNN's lower raw throughput reflects the impact of routing very sparse
tiles through its fused selective path~\cite{deltacnn}. The two YOLO workloads
lie at the upper end
because their higher active ratios expose more parallelism and amortize fixed
costs.

\sysname's useful-compute ratio is 98.9\%,
96.2\%, and 95.2\% for FireFlowNet, YOLOv8n, and YOLOv8m.
DynConv is lower at 81.7\%; its low active ratio and small spatial grids yield
exceptionally short worklists, with P10, P50, and P90 lengths of 3, 43, and 389.
Padding in the issued position sequence $\mathcal{C}$ is therefore difficult
to avoid. DynConv's layer-specific gates also provide no shared activity signal
across layers, and the evaluated MPII~\cite{mpii} samples are not temporally
related and thus cannot support lagged prediction; yet its useful-compute ratio
remains well above tile skipping's 48.9\%.
Combining throughput and useful-compute ratio, \sysname achieves the
highest effective throughput in every workload, reaching 1.51--1.86$\times$
that of the strongest competing path.

Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency} localizes the remaining wasted work:
despite cross-operator reuse, $\eta_{\mathrm{construct}}$ remains at
98.5--100.0\%. The selected decompositions give $\eta_{\mathrm{compute}}$
values of 99.1\%, 97.7\%, and 96.5\% for FireFlowNet, YOLOv8n, and YOLOv8m;
because DynConv has $\eta_{\mathrm{construct}}=100.0\%$, its
lower $\eta$ comes entirely from compute-stage padding of its short worklists.

\paragraph{Latency cost decomposition.}
We decompose full-model YOLOv8n latency on RTX~3080 by execution stage
(Table~\ref{tab:cost-decomposition}), where frames from MOT16~\cite{mot16}
are classified into bins of low or high active ratios. \sysname's construction
time remains at 0.105--0.106\,ms while convolution grows from 1.09 to
1.29\,ms across the two bins; construction's latency share consequently falls
from 7.1\% to 6.2\%. Gather-scatter
shows why exact selection alone is insufficient: it has the lowest convolution
time at 0.73--0.83\,ms, but extraction, setup, and writeback put
10.9--11.0\,ms (92.2--93.1\% of total latency) in \emph{Other}. \sysname
instead spends 0.160--0.165\,ms (9.8--10.9\% of latency) on \emph{Other}, with
convolution dominant at 73.8--76.3\%; direct worklist consumption avoids the
gather-scatter path's data-movement bottleneck.
