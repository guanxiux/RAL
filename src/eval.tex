\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

\paragraph{Platforms.}

We evaluate six GPU platforms spanning four discrete GPUs and two embedded
SoCs. The discrete platforms are RTX~4070 Laptop, RTX~3080,
\textit{TBD Discrete GPU A}, and \textit{TBD Discrete GPU B}.
They span laptop- and desktop-class devices and test \sysname across commodity
GPU performance tiers. Jetson Xavier NX and Jetson AGX Orin complement this
coverage with resource- and power-constrained deployments, where we also
measure board energy.

\paragraph{Models, tasks, and mask sources.}
Our workloads span three vision tasks.

\noindent\emph{Event-based optical flow.}
We evaluate FireFlowNet~\cite{fireflownet}, a lightweight event-based
optical-flow model that delivers high inference rates while retaining
competitive accuracy, on MVSEC~\cite{mvsec}. MVSEC contains event-camera
sequences and corresponding ground-truth flow from indoor quadrotor flight and
outdoor driving. Following Ev-Conv~\cite{evconv}, consecutive 50-ms windows
advance by 1~ms; events entering or leaving the window form the sparse
increment from which we derive layer masks.

\noindent\emph{Detection.}
We evaluate YOLOv8n and YOLOv8m~\cite{yolov8} on MOT16~\cite{mot16}, a
collection of crowded pedestrian videos for multi-object tracking. Their
two model sizes expose how scale affects sparse-execution efficiency.
For both models, DeltaCNN's temporal policy~\cite{deltacnn} thresholds
activation differences between successive frames and propagates the resulting
masks across layers.

\noindent\emph{Human pose estimation.}
We use the DynConv pose model~\cite{dynconv} on MPII~\cite{mpii}, which depicts
people performing diverse activities and annotates their 2D body joints.
DynConv's learned spatial gates produce an input-dependent mask for each gated
block.


Table~\ref{tab:models} summarizes the workloads and evaluation scales.
For every workload, calibration uses the training split, while all reported
results use a disjoint test split. We use
the authors' released weights for all four models~\cite{fireflownet,yolov8,dynconv}.
Within each workload, we fix the inputs, weights, and upstream mask policy;
every sparse path receives the same resulting layer masks, isolating the
masked-convolution backend.

\paragraph{Baselines.}

We compare \sysname against three FP32 execution paths established by prior
work:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dense} executes the complete model with the native
        PyTorch/cuDNN backend~\cite{cudnn}.

  \item \textbf{Tile skipping} retains the dense tiled pipeline and skips a
        tile only when all its outputs are inactive, following SBNet, Ev-Conv, and
        DeltaCNN~\cite{sbnet,evconv,deltacnn}.

  \item \textbf{Gather-scatter} uses DynConv's original CUDA backend for
        pose~\cite{dynconv}. FireFlowNet and YOLO use an adaptation of MMCV's
        masked-convolution operator~\cite{mmcv}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Measurements.}

We measure per-frame full-model GPU latency over the complete evaluation data
above with CUDA events; every sequence or split is repeated three times. Timing
covers mask propagation, worklist construction, all network operators, and
output update; it excludes input transfer, offline calibration and autotuning,
and one-time state initialization. On the Jetson platforms, we integrate
board-level \texttt{VDD\_IN} over each timed round to obtain its mean power,
multiply that value by the round's cumulative CUDA-event time, and aggregate
across rounds to obtain per-frame board energy. We follow the original workload
protocols: AEE for optical flow~\cite{evconv}, COCO-style average precision
(AP) for detection, averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to
0.95~\cite{deltacnn}, and PCKh for human pose~\cite{dynconv}. Because
MOT16 releases ground-truth detections only for its training split, AP on the
test sequences uses fixed pseudo-labels from a dense YOLO26x
teacher~\cite{yolo26}.

\subsection{End-to-End Performance}
\label{sec:eval:end-to-end}

\sysname is the fastest path in all 16 currently measured workload-platform
pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}). Relative to the fastest competing path
for each pair, it reduces full-model latency by 22.1--46.5\%. The reduction is
34.2--46.5\% on the discrete GPUs and 22.1--38.6\% on the Jetson platforms,
showing that the benefit persists across both discrete GPUs and embedded SoCs.
Existing sparse paths are inconsistent: even
the faster of tile skipping and gather-scatter fails to beat dense execution
in 8 of the 16 pairs. This outcome reflects the tension established in
\S\ref{sec:bg:gap}: reducing scheduled work does not guarantee lower latency
when the execution path sacrifices throughput.

These latency savings also translate into lower board energy. \sysname has the
lowest per-frame energy in every Jetson workload-platform pair
(Table~\ref{tab:e2e-energy}), reducing energy by
17.0--39.8\% relative to the fastest competing path and by 28.5--72.6\%
relative to dense execution.

Task accuracy remains determined by the upstream mask policy rather than the
execution backend. Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-accuracy} reports each workload once on
RTX~3080 because all platforms use the same weights, inputs, and masks. The
three sparse paths agree within 0.31 AP points for detection and to the shown
precision for optical flow and pose. Relative to dense execution, the selected
mask policies change accuracy by at most 3.0 AP points for detection and 1.4
PCKh points for pose, while slightly improving FireFlowNet's AEE. Thus the
latency and energy comparisons do not exchange backend correctness for speed.
