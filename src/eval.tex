\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

We ask three questions: whether \sysname turns spatial sparsity into
end-to-end latency and energy reduction across GPU classes
(\S\ref{sec:eval:end-to-end}), where its remaining time goes
(\S\ref{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}), and whether each co-designed component is
necessary (\S\ref{sec:eval:ablation-sensitivity}).

\paragraph{Platforms.}
We evaluate six NVIDIA GPUs that span datacenter, desktop, laptop, and
embedded classes: A100, RTX~4090, RTX~3080, RTX~4070 Laptop, Jetson AGX Orin,
and Jetson Xavier NX. GPU clocks are locked on every platform so that
latencies are stable across runs. On the two Jetson boards, where the energy
budget constrains deployment, we also report per-frame energy from the board's
input power.

\paragraph{Workloads.}
The three workloads (Table~\ref{tab:workloads}) cover the three mask sources
of \S\ref{sec:bg:masked}: event masks, temporal-difference masks, and learned
spatial gates. Each mask policy follows its upstream method; where it needs
thresholds, they are tuned on data disjoint from the evaluation set.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
\emph{Dense} runs the original model with PyTorch and cuDNN~\cite{cudnn}
autotuning: it computes every position and carries none of the masking
machinery (no mask generation, propagation, or temporal state).
\emph{Tile skipping} uses DeltaCNN's kernels~\cite{deltacnn}, and
\emph{gather-scatter} uses MMCV's masked convolution~\cite{mmcv} (DynConv's
own CUDA backend~\cite{dynconv} for pose). All paths run the same weights and
inputs, and the three masked paths the same layer masks, so they differ only
in the masked-convolution backend. Every path except gather-scatter replays as
a CUDA graph; gather-scatter reads each layer's active count on the host to
size its buffers, so it must run eagerly. The baselines implement only FP32, so
FP16 compares \sysname with Dense alone. We report \sysname's speedup over the
fastest baseline on each workload and platform.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Latency is the GPU time of one frame measured with CUDA events; it includes
worklist construction and all non-convolution operators. YOLOv8m processes whole videos continuously, so its
temporal masks evolve as in deployment, at batch~1 (YOLOv8m b1: a single
latency-bound camera, our primary setting) and batch~4 (YOLOv8m b4: four
concurrent camera streams).
\sysname's per-device tuning (\S\ref{sec:design:select}) runs once offline
and is excluded. Task accuracy is reported to confirm that the sparse paths
agree.

\paragraph{Latency.}
\sysname is the fastest path in all 44 groups of workload, platform, and
precision (Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}). In FP32 it runs 1.49--3.23$\times$
faster than the fastest baseline of each group (geometric mean 2.06$\times$),
and in FP16, where Dense is the only baseline, 1.27--4.25$\times$ faster
(2.10$\times$), 2.08$\times$ over all 44 groups. No baseline is consistently
second: tile skipping is the
fastest baseline in 13 of the 24 FP32 groups, gather-scatter in 6, and Dense
in 5. Gather-scatter varies most, because its per-layer extraction, scatter,
and host synchronization do not shrink with the active ratio. In 12 FP32
groups, mostly FireFlowNet and DynConv, it runs 1.85--12.1$\times$ slower than
Dense and extends beyond the axis (red crosses). On YOLOv8m b4, four streams
amortize these costs and make it the fastest baseline on four of the six GPUs,
where \sysname still runs 1.69--2.39$\times$ faster.

The distance to the ideal line follows worklist length, as Insight~4
predicts. Averaged over GPUs and precisions, \sysname runs at 1.61$\times$ the
ideal latency on YOLOv8m b4, whose median worklist holds 1{,}382 entries, and
at 2.78$\times$ on DynConv, whose median worklist holds 43
(Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency}), so per-layer fixed costs dominate its
frame.

\paragraph{Energy.}
Shorter frames translate into lower energy (Table~\ref{tab:e2e-energy}).
\sysname is the lowest-energy path for every workload on both Jetson boards:
in FP32 it uses 32.8--51.8\% less energy per frame than the lowest-energy
baseline, and across both precisions 60.8--82.5\% less than Dense.

\input{figures/e2e_energy_table}

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
Given the same layer masks, the three masked paths reach the same accuracy
(Table~\ref{tab:e2e-accuracy}): AEE and PCKh are identical, and YOLOv8m AP
differs by at most 0.1 points, a residual of floating-point reduction order.
Where worklist reuse computes a superset of the required positions, the output
mask gates writeback, so the extra positions never reach downstream layers.
The remaining gap to Dense (1.4 PCKh points lower on DynConv, slightly better
AEE and AP elsewhere) is set by the upstream mask policy.

\label{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}

  \label{fig:effective-throughput}

\paragraph{Effective throughput.}
Figure~\ref{fig:effective-throughput} explains the end-to-end result with the
two axes of Eq.~\ref{eq:efficiency-framework-throughput}: a masked path is
fast only if it is both selective (useful-compute ratio $\eta$) and keeps
dense throughput ($T$). The two baselines fail on opposite axes. Tile skipping
keeps the dense tile schedule, reaching 17.5--59.9\% of Dense raw throughput,
but computes every position of an active tile, so $\eta$ is 0.49--0.74.
Gather-scatter issues only required positions ($\eta = 1$), but extraction and
writeback hold its raw throughput to 2.6--42.0\% of Dense. \sysname keeps
$\eta$ at 0.91--1.00 and sustains 52.3--76.0\% of Dense raw throughput in
FP32 (29.6--76.0\% in FP16), the highest effective throughput in every group.

\input{figures/worklist_efficiency_table}

Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency} locates \sysname's remaining loss with the
decomposition of Eq.~\ref{eq:eta-decomp}. Tile-local compaction emits each
active coordinate exactly once ($\eta_{\mathrm{construct}} = 100\%$), so all
loss is padding in the final tile of each worklist ($\eta_{\mathrm{compute}}$)
and it follows worklist length: 99.8\% and 98.1\% on FireFlowNet and YOLOv8m
b4, whose median worklists hold thousands of entries, and 93.6\% and 92.1\% on
YOLOv8m b1 and DynConv, whose shortest worklists (P10) hold 26 and 3 entries.

\paragraph{Latency decomposition.}
Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition} shows where each path spends a YOLOv8m frame.
At b1 in FP32, \sysname cuts convolution from 7.21 to 2.27\,ms at the cost of
0.17\,ms of construction, and keeps the GPU busy because the compute path never
returns to the host. Gather-scatter spends the least time in convolution (1.68\,ms),
but its per-layer host synchronization leaves the GPU idle for 8.26\,ms, 64.8\%
of its frame; at b4, four streams hide most of that idle time and bring it to
4.50\,ms per frame, still 2.39$\times$ \sysname's 1.88\,ms. In FP16, tensor
cores shrink convolution more than the rest of the frame: construction and
other operators grow from 24.3\% to 39.0\% of \sysname's b1 frame, and its
speedup over Dense narrows from 2.79$\times$ to 2.53$\times$.

\paragraph{Speedup across activity.}
Grouping the frames of YOLOv8m b1 on RTX~3080 by their active ratio tests the
affine behavior end to end. \sysname's speedup over Dense falls smoothly with
activity, from 4.59$\times$ for frames with $\alpha<0.02$ to 1.31$\times$ for
$\alpha\ge0.4$ in FP32 (3.14$\times$ to 1.43$\times$ in FP16), as the affine
model with a per-layer intercept predicts (Insights~1 and~4). Neither baseline
turns low activity into a speedup: both run slower than Dense at every active
ratio. The selection of \S\ref{sec:design:select} places
its gains where the uniform prior expects them: against the speedup-optimal
configuration, \sysname is 1.13$\times$ faster on the sparsest frames and at
most 4\% slower on the densest (FP32).

\paragraph{Memory overhead.}
\sysname's metadata adds little memory (Table~\ref{tab:memory}). The temporal
mask policies of FireFlowNet and YOLOv8m retain previous-frame activations, so
every masked path allocates 3.8--6.3$\times$ Dense, and \sysname stays within
the range of the other masked paths (128.6 vs.\ 141.8--197.0\,MiB on
FireFlowNet). DynConv's learned gates keep no temporal state, and \sysname
allocates 0.1\,MiB more than Dense.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:eval:ablation-sensitivity}

\input{figures/ablation_table}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} replaces one mechanism at a time on YOLOv8m (b1,
RTX~3080, FP16). Rebuilding the worklist at every layer (No reuse) doubles
construction time from 0.16 to 0.30\,ms and adds 10\% to frame latency. The
shuffled-order and full-layer-grid variants modify the speedup-optimal
configuration and are compared against it. A shuffled worklist costs 3\%, as
Insight~3 predicts (\S\ref{sec:char:order}). Launching the dense layer's grid,
whose CTAs beyond the worklist exit, raises convolution time from 0.88 to
1.06\,ms (1.13$\times$), the cost that the persistent grid avoids
(\S\ref{sec:design:compute}). Reading $n_W$ back to the host at every layer to
launch the exact grid runs 6.51$\times$ slower, as Insight~5 predicts. For
selection, the configuration fastest at $\alpha = 1$ runs 1.71$\times$ slower,
and the single configurations with the best expected speedup
(speedup-optimal) or the lowest expected latency (latency-optimal) over a
uniform $n_W$ run 1.02$\times$ and 1.10$\times$ slower.
Placing four candidates at every layer regardless of their cost
(Cost-unaware $K_{\max}$) runs 1.07$\times$ slower: more producers take the
selection path, raising construction time to 0.22\,ms. The trade-off instead
sets $K$ per GPU and precision: on YOLOv8m, RTX~3080 follows the length on 5
of 83 layers in FP16 and 30 in FP32, and AGX Orin, whose gains are larger, on
62 in FP32. Only the zero-cost
oracle, which runs the same candidates on a graph captured for each frame's own
$n_W$ without any selection mechanism, beats \sysname, by 8\%; this margin is
the selection cost that current GPUs impose (\S\ref{sec:char:cost}).

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\sysname converts spatial sparsity into latency, so its benefit is bounded by
how sparse the upstream masks are and by how much of the frame convolution
occupies. First, the gain vanishes as the active ratio approaches one: at full
activity the per-length best configuration only matches dense execution
(\S\ref{sec:char:affine}) while construction still runs, so a fully active
input runs slightly slower than the dense path. \sysname consumes masks and
makes no claim about their quality or the resulting accuracy; a policy that
keeps most positions active should stay on the dense path. Second, \sysname
accelerates convolution only, so convolution's share of frame latency caps the
end-to-end speedup. FP16 tensor cores shrink that share, and the remaining
non-convolution operators become the next bottleneck, which is why FP16
speedups are narrower than FP32 ones (\S\ref{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}).
Models dominated by other operators bound the gain further.

\paragraph{Hardware implications.}
The characterization traces the remaining gap to two per-layer costs that
current GPUs leave to software. The intercept of the affine model is launch
and dependency latency rather than lost bandwidth (Insight~4), and its share
grows as inputs become sparser; hardware that shortens the path from launch to
the first useful instruction (for example, by staging kernel parameters before
the CTA starts) and the dependent chain of a single tile would move sparse
execution toward the proportional line. Following the worklist length costs
about as much per decision point as it gains (Insight~5), because every
existing mechanism either returns to the host or reconfigures the graph. A
launch predicate that the hardware evaluates on a device-resident value before
allocating CTA resources, or a work distributor that picks the tile shape from
the worklist length, would let every layer adapt its configuration at
negligible cost.
