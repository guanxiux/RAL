\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

\paragraph{Platforms.}

We evaluate six GPU platforms. The four discrete GPUs are NVIDIA A100,
RTX~4090, RTX~3080, and RTX~4070 Laptop. The embedded platforms are Jetson
Xavier NX and AGX Orin; we also measure board energy on both. The discrete
platforms use PyTorch 2.1.2, CUDA 12.1, and cuDNN 8.9.2; both Jetson platforms
use PyTorch 2.1.0, CUDA 11.4, and cuDNN 8.6. All paths in the full workload
comparison use FP32 (SIMT) with TF32 disabled. Sensitivity studies additionally
evaluate FP16 (tensor core) on selected platforms. On each platform, all backends run under the same fixed clock and
power configuration.

\paragraph{Models, tasks, and mask sources.}
Our workloads (Table~\ref{tab:models}) span three vision tasks:
FireFlowNet~\cite{fireflownet} for event-based optical flow on
MVSEC~\cite{mvsec} with Ev-Conv~\cite{evconv} masks,
YOLOv8n and YOLOv8m~\cite{yolov8} for detection on MOT16~\cite{mot16} with
DeltaCNN's~\cite{deltacnn} temporal masks, and
DynConv~\cite{dynconv} for human pose on MPII~\cite{mpii} with learned spatial
gates. For every workload, calibration uses the training split, while all
reported results use a disjoint test split with the authors' released
weights~\cite{fireflownet,yolov8,dynconv}. Within each workload, we fix
inputs, weights, and upstream mask policy; every sparse path receives the same
layer masks, isolating the masked-convolution backend.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare \sysname against three FP32 (SIMT) execution paths:
\textbf{Dense} (PyTorch/cuDNN~\cite{cudnn} with autotuning),
\textbf{Tile skipping} (DeltaCNN~\cite{deltacnn} for FireFlowNet and YOLO;
DynConv supplies output masks directly), and
\textbf{Gather-scatter} (DynConv's~\cite{dynconv} CUDA backend for pose;
MMCV's~\cite{mmcv} MaskedConv2d for FireFlowNet and YOLO).

\paragraph{Measurements.}

We measure per-frame full-model GPU latency over the complete evaluation
datasets. CUDA events bracket each frame's inference; after warm-up we run
three complete rounds and average per-frame latency across all frames and
rounds. The interval includes mask propagation, worklist construction, all
network operators, and output update; it excludes input transfer, offline
calibration, and one-time initialization. We report $\eta$, $T$, and
$T_{\mathrm{eff}}$ at the model level by accumulating required and issued
work across layers per frame (Eq.~\ref{eq:efficiency-framework}), then
averaging across frames. On the Jetson platforms, we integrate board-level
\texttt{VDD\_IN} power over each frame interval to obtain per-frame energy.
For accuracy, we follow the original protocols: AEE for optical
flow~\cite{evconv}, AP for detection~\cite{deltacnn}, and PCKh for
pose~\cite{dynconv}. MOT16 AP uses fixed pseudo-labels from a dense YOLO26x
teacher~\cite{yolo26} because ground-truth detections cover only the training
split.

\paragraph{Latency.}
\sysname is the fastest path in all 24
measured workload-platform pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}). Relative to
the fastest evaluated competing path in each pair, it achieves a 1.58$\times$
geometric-mean speedup; individual-pair speedups range from 1.33$\times$ to
2.12$\times$, corresponding to 25.0--52.8\% lower latency. Gains span more
than two orders of magnitude in absolute latency, from 0.23\,ms for
FireFlowNet on RTX~4090 to 88.6\,ms for YOLOv8m on Xavier NX.

The two existing sparse paths split the remaining 24 pairs between them: dense
is fastest in 13 and tile skipping in 11; gather-scatter never wins. The split
tracks active ratio: at the YOLO workloads' $\alpha\approx48$--$49\%$, few
tiles are fully inactive, so tile skipping cannot compensate for its lower
throughput; on FireFlowNet ($\alpha\approx25\%$) and DynConv
($\alpha\approx16\%$), enough tiles are skippable for it to overtake dense.
Neither path is uniformly strong, and \sysname outperforms whichever wins.
On the Jetson platforms the lead narrows
(geometric mean 1.53$\times$ vs.\ 1.61$\times$ on discrete GPUs) but \sysname
remains fastest in all eight pairs.

Across all 24 pairs, \sysname averages 1.76$\times$ the ideal
proportional-scaling reference (dashed lines in
Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}), compared with 2.79$\times$ for the per-pair
fastest competing path. The gap to ideal tracks active ratio: on the YOLO
workloads \sysname runs at 1.36$\times$ ideal, while on DynConv
($\alpha\approx16\%$) construction and fixed costs widen it to
2.60$\times$.

\paragraph{Energy.}
\sysname is the lowest-energy path for every workload on both Jetson
platforms (Table~\ref{tab:e2e-energy}), reducing energy by
9.3--36.1\% relative to the lowest-energy competing path in each pair and by
29.9--67.8\% relative to dense execution.

\sysname's latency and energy gains do not alter accuracy.
\sysname matches the other sparse paths on every workload: AEE and PCKh are
identical and YOLOv8 AP differs by at most 0.14 points, a residual of
floating-point reduction order rather than any difference in covered positions
(Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-accuracy}); even where worklist reuse computes a superset
of the required positions, the output mask gates writeback so the extra
slots do not reach downstream layers. The remaining accuracy gap between
sparse and dense, within 4\% across all workloads, is set by the upstream
mask policy, which \sysname consumes without modification.


\subsection{Microbenchmarks}
\label{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}

\input{figures/worklist_efficiency_table}

\paragraph{Effective throughput.}
A sparse path lowers latency only if it is both selective, captured by the
useful-compute ratio $\eta$, and fast, captured by raw throughput $T$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:efficiency-framework-throughput}). Figure~\ref{fig:effective-throughput}
reports both, normalized to Dense, on RTX~3080 and AGX~Orin, and the two existing
paths fail on opposite axes. Tile skipping retains the dense tile schedule and so
keeps 41--65\% of Dense raw throughput, but an active tile computes every
position it covers: its useful-compute ratio falls to 0.45--0.72, wasting
28--55\% of the slots it issues. Gather-scatter issues exactly the required
positions, giving $\eta=1$, but extraction and writeback collapse its
throughput to 2--47\% of Dense. \sysname is the only path that is high on both
axes, sustaining $\eta=0.90$--$1.00$ and the highest raw throughput among the
sparse paths in every workload---42.0--85.1\% of Dense on RTX~3080 and
51.3--86.8\% on AGX~Orin. The two YOLO workloads lie at the upper end because
their higher active ratios expose more parallelism and amortize fixed costs;
DeltaCNN's throughput on FireFlowNet and DynConv reflects routing very sparse
tiles through its fused selective path~\cite{deltacnn}. Because effective
throughput is the product of the two axes, \sysname reaches 1.38--2.06$\times$
the strongest competing effective throughput on RTX~3080 and 1.47--1.87$\times$
on AGX~Orin.

Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency} localizes \sysname's own loss on RTX~3080
through the decomposition of Eq.~\ref{eq:eta-decomp}. $\eta_{\mathrm{construct}}$,
the fraction of worklist entries that are required positions, stays at
98.6--100.0\%: tile-local compaction emits each active coordinate exactly once,
and only reuse of a superset worklist gives up the remainder.
$\eta_{\mathrm{compute}}$, the fraction of issued slots that are worklist
entries, exceeds 99.5\% for FireFlowNet and the two YOLO workloads. DynConv is
the exception at 90.6\%: its low active ratio and small spatial grids yield
exceptionally short worklists (P10, P50, and P90 lengths of 3, 43, and 389), so
$B_M$-sized final tiles are mostly padding. Because its inputs are temporally
unrelated, DynConv uses the same exact-length device policy without a temporal
activity selector. Even at this worst case the total $\eta$ is more than double
tile skipping's 44.8\% on the same workload.

\paragraph{Latency cost decomposition.}
We decompose full-model YOLOv8n latency by execution stage on RTX~3080 and AGX
Orin (Table~\ref{tab:cost-decomposition}). On RTX~3080, \sysname reduces the
convolution share from 2.32 to 0.99\,ms (2.3$\times$, close to the
$1/\alpha\approx2.2$ proportional target) while adding 0.12\,ms of
construction, lowering the total from 2.82 to 1.66\,ms. On AGX~Orin the
convolution reduction is 2.0$\times$ (11.91 to 6.06\,ms) at 0.51\,ms of
construction, and the total falls from 15.45 to 10.22\,ms.

Gather-scatter is the exception: it issues exactly the required positions, so its
convolution is the smallest share of any path (30\% of its total), but extraction,
scatter, and buffer setup cost 7.48\,ms, 59\% of its total, on RTX~3080. Exact
selection alone does not lower latency without a fused datapath.

\paragraph{Memory overhead.}
Table~\ref{tab:memory} reports peak live GPU allocation.
FireFlowNet, YOLOv8n, and YOLOv8m use temporal mask policies that retain
previous-frame activations for differencing, so every sparse path allocates
3--4$\times$ Dense. The temporal state dominates the gap, and \sysname stays
within the same tier as tile skipping; gather-scatter is the exception on
YOLOv8m, where materializing input patches for each active position adds to the
temporal state and pushes it above both other sparse paths.
DynConv applies learned spatial gates without temporal state, placing all
backends in a narrow 27--34\,MiB range. Here \sysname's worklist and coordinate
metadata add only 2.3\,MiB over Dense, while gather-scatter is the most compact
because DynConv's predominantly pointwise block structure lets intermediates
stay in the gathered compact representation~\cite{dynconv}.

\subsection{Ablation Study and Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:eval:ablation-sensitivity}

\input{figures/ablation_table}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates each co-designed component by disabling it
while holding all others fixed on YOLOv8n; the stage decomposition separates
the effect on construction and convolution.

\emph{Construction.} On RTX~3080, Atomic append and Global order raise construction from
0.12 to 0.43 and 0.54\,ms without reducing convolution (0.94\,ms throughout),
adding 19.1\% and 19.6\% to the end-to-end latency over Ours; Global order's
higher construction reflects its additional kernel passes. No reuse
\emph{raises} $\eta$ to 99.7\% ($\eta_{\mathrm{construct}}=1$ per layer) but
increases construction 1.6$\times$ (0.12 to 0.19\,ms), adding 5.7\% over Ours:
rebuilding the worklist every layer is more expensive than reusing a slightly loose
superset worklist. On AGX~Orin the same pattern holds with smaller margins:
construction variants add 1--9\% over Ours while convolution stays within
0.05\,ms, because convolution is a larger share of the total.

\emph{Compute.} On RTX~3080, DP only raises convolution from 0.94 to 1.01\,ms over Ours
(+0.07\,ms); No hybrid, which retains full Stream-$K$ but not the hybrid
mode, recovers most of that gap (0.96\,ms, +0.02\,ms over Ours). Readback \&
launch runs the same kernels as Ours but adds per-operator host
synchronization, inflating the total from 1.66 to 7.61\,ms (4.58$\times$
Ours). On AGX~Orin, DP only adds 1.5\% end-to-end latency over Ours and
readback inflates it to 2.38$\times$ Ours.

\paragraph{Tensor core sensitivity.}
Figure~\ref{fig:dtype-sensitivity} compares FP32 and FP16 execution on
RTX~3080 and AGX~Orin. The evaluated sparse
baselines~\cite{deltacnn,dynconv,mmcv} provide only FP32 paths, so this
study compares Dense and \sysname only. \sysname retains a 1.07--1.99$\times$
speedup over Dense under FP16 tensor cores across all eight workload-platform
pairs, narrowing from 1.51--3.04$\times$ under FP32. The narrowing is
expected: tensor cores reduce the convolution time that \sysname saves by
skipping, shrinking the absolute saving while non-convolution costs
(construction, elementwise, mask processing) do not shrink proportionally. The two YOLO
workloads narrow the most (1.51--1.70$\times$ under FP32 to
1.07--1.37$\times$ under FP16) because their high active ratio
($\alpha\approx48$--$49\%$) already limits the sparsity gain; within the two,
YOLOv8n narrows further because its smaller model makes non-convolution costs
a larger share of the total under FP16.

\input{figures/batch_cost_decomposition_table}

\paragraph{Stream-level batching.}
Table~\ref{tab:batch-cost-decomposition} reports per-frame latency
decomposition for YOLOv8n on RTX~3080 at batch sizes 1, 4, and~8. \sysname
remains fastest at every batch size, but its lead over the fastest competing
path narrows from 1.70$\times$ at batch~1 to 1.59$\times$ at batch~8. Tile
skipping benefits most: from batch~1 to~8, its per-frame convolution drops from
2.89 to 0.92\,ms, compared with 0.99 to 0.49\,ms for \sysname, because batching adds
independent tiles that fill otherwise idle SMs~\cite{deltacnn}, the same
occupancy that \sysname's adaptive decomposition already provides at batch~1.
Tile skipping accordingly overtakes dense at batch~4 and stays the fastest
competing path at batch~8. Gather-scatter's extraction overhead does not
amortize: \emph{Other} still accounts for 59.4\% of its per-frame latency at
batch~8.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

\sysname consumes rather than generates masks, so its gains are conditional on
the upstream policy and its activity distribution; it guarantees coverage of
requested outputs but does not improve mask quality. When the active ratio is
high enough, construction overhead and the gap between the worklist kernel and
cuDNN leave little room for a net gain, as the YOLO workloads' narrower
speedups already suggest.

The adaptive decomposition is calibrated per convolution shape and device;
results are cached and reused across models that share the same shapes, but a
new shape or device requires reprofiling. Because calibration selects only
among decomposition modes, it affects performance but not coverage or
correctness. The current calibration policy is simple; a faster or more
effective one is a natural direction for future work.

The full workload comparison uses FP32 (SIMT) because the evaluated sparse
baselines~\cite{deltacnn,dynconv,mmcv} provide only FP32 paths. The
sensitivity study confirms that \sysname retains end-to-end gains under FP16
tensor cores on both evaluated platforms across all four workloads.
Extending the worklist compute stage to native tensor-core reduction remains
open. Stream-$K$ performs FP32 partial-sum reduction through an external
workspace (\S\ref{sec:design:compute}), which is necessary for numerical
precision but adds overhead that, for the short worklists where split-$K$ is
most needed, can outweigh the tensor-core arithmetic savings. A broader
study across GPU architectures remains for future work.
