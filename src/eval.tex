\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

\paragraph{Platforms.}

We evaluate six GPU platforms spanning four discrete GPUs and two embedded
SoCs. The discrete platforms are NVIDIA A100, RTX~4090, RTX~3080, and
RTX~4070 Laptop.
The four discrete GPUs cover a range of compute capabilities. Jetson Xavier NX
and Jetson AGX Orin extend the evaluation to embedded SoCs with limited compute
and power budgets, where we also measure board energy.

\paragraph{Models, tasks, and mask sources.}
Our workloads span three vision tasks.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Event-based optical flow.}
        FireFlowNet~\cite{fireflownet} is a lightweight event-based optical-flow
        model that delivers high inference rates while retaining competitive
        accuracy. We evaluate it on MVSEC~\cite{mvsec}, which contains
        event-camera sequences and corresponding ground-truth flow from indoor
        quadrotor flight and outdoor driving. Following Ev-Conv~\cite{evconv},
        consecutive 50-ms windows advance by 1~ms; events entering or leaving
        the window form the sparse increment from which we derive layer masks.

  \item \textbf{Detection.} We evaluate YOLOv8n and
        YOLOv8m~\cite{yolov8} on MOT16~\cite{mot16}, a collection of crowded
        pedestrian videos for multi-object tracking. Their two model sizes
        expose how scale affects sparse-execution efficiency. For both models,
        DeltaCNN's temporal policy~\cite{deltacnn} thresholds activation
        differences between successive frames and propagates the resulting
        masks across layers.

  \item \textbf{Human pose estimation.} We use the DynConv pose
        model~\cite{dynconv} on MPII~\cite{mpii}, which depicts people
        performing diverse activities and annotates their 2D body joints.
        DynConv's learned spatial gates produce an input-dependent mask for
        each gated block.
\end{itemize}


Table~\ref{tab:models} summarizes the workloads and evaluation scales.
For every workload, calibration uses the training split, while all reported
results use a disjoint test split. We use
the authors' released weights for all four models~\cite{fireflownet,yolov8,dynconv}.
Within each workload, we fix the inputs, weights, and upstream mask policy;
every sparse path receives the same resulting layer masks, isolating the
masked-convolution backend.

\paragraph{Baselines.}

We compare \sysname against three FP32 execution paths established by prior
work:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dense} executes the complete model with the native
        PyTorch/cuDNN backend~\cite{cudnn}.

  \item \textbf{Tile skipping} uses DeltaCNN's default execution path for
        FireFlowNet (with Ev-Conv) and YOLO~\cite{deltacnn}. DynConv directly
        provides the output mask. We therefore bypass only mask propagation;
        its tile-skipping path is unchanged.

  \item \textbf{Gather-scatter} uses DynConv's original CUDA backend for
        pose~\cite{dynconv}; FireFlowNet and YOLO use MMCV's masked-convolution
        path~\cite{mmcv}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Measurements.}

We measure per-frame full-model GPU latency over the complete evaluation data
above with CUDA events that bracket the full inference call; every sequence or
split is repeated three times. The bracketed call includes nonblocking activity
polling and bucket lookup, mask propagation, worklist construction, all network
operators, and output update; it excludes input transfer, offline calibration
and autotuning, and one-time state initialization. Since we focus on end-to-end performance,
we report $\eta$, $T$, and $T_{\mathrm{eff}}$ at the model level by
accumulating required and issued convolution work across all layers within each
frame before applying Eq.~\ref{eq:efficiency-framework}, then averaging the
frame-level values across frames. On the Jetson platforms, we integrate
board-level \texttt{VDD\_IN} over each timed round to obtain per-frame board energy. We follow the original workload
protocols: AEE for optical flow~\cite{evconv}, COCO-style average precision
(AP) for detection, averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to
0.95~\cite{deltacnn}, and PCKh for human pose~\cite{dynconv}. Because
MOT16 releases ground-truth detections only for its training split, AP on the
test sequences uses fixed pseudo-labels from a dense YOLO26x
teacher~\cite{yolo26}.

\paragraph{Latency.}
\sysname consistently converts upstream sparsity into lower full-model latency
across all evaluated workloads and platforms. It is the fastest path in all 24
measured workload-platform pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-latency}). Relative to
the fastest evaluated competing path in each pair, it achieves a 1.57$\times$
geometric-mean speedup; individual-pair speedups range from 1.28$\times$ to
1.87$\times$, corresponding to 22.1--46.5\% lower latency. These gains persist
despite a more than two-order-of-magnitude span in absolute latency, from
0.309\,ms for FireFlowNet on RTX~4090 to 94.3\,ms for YOLOv8m on Xavier NX.

Unlike existing sparse paths, \sysname delivers uniform gains across workload
regimes. Dense is the fastest evaluated competing path in 15 of the 24
pairs, compared with eight for tile skipping and only one for gather-scatter.
FireFlowNet's low required-work ratio gives tile skipping enough headroom to
beat Dense on five of six platforms and reduce its latency by up to 42.0\%.
The higher required-work ratios of YOLOv8n and YOLOv8m leave less removable
work: tile skipping reaches only near parity with Dense on AGX Orin and is
slower in the other ten YOLO pairs. Gather-scatter becomes the fastest
competing path only for YOLOv8m on AGX Orin, where the heavier convolution
amortizes extraction and writeback. DynConv exposes the other limit: despite
having the lowest required-work ratio, tile skipping beats Dense only on the
two Jetson platforms. Sparsity magnitude alone therefore does not determine
where an existing sparse path crosses the dense baseline.

Existing sparse paths are substantially more competitive with Dense on the
Jetson devices than on the discrete GPUs. The fastest existing sparse path has
lower measured latency than Dense in six of the eight Jetson pairs, but in
only three of the 16 discrete-GPU pairs; its geometric-mean speedup over Dense
is 1.26$\times$ on Jetson and 0.80$\times$ on the discrete GPUs. This trend is
consistent with differential scaling between dense and selective execution.
On the lower-throughput, narrower Jetson GPUs, avoiding dense-tile work can
save more wall-clock time relative to selective-path overhead, while shorter
sparse schedules incur less underfill than on wider discrete GPUs. The effect
does not make sparse execution uniformly effective: gather-scatter exceeds
Dense in only one pair, and both existing sparse paths remain slower than Dense
for the two YOLO models on Xavier NX. These stronger embedded baselines narrow
\sysname's geometric-mean lead over the per-pair fastest evaluated competing path from
1.62$\times$ on the discrete GPUs to 1.48$\times$ on Jetson, yet \sysname
remains fastest in all eight Jetson pairs.

\sysname also realizes more of the available sparsity headroom than every
competing path. It is the closest path to the ideal proportional-scaling
reference in every pair, running at 1.09--4.18$\times$ the reference while the
fastest evaluated competing path remains at 1.88--6.43$\times$. The remaining gap
reflects work that does not shrink with convolution activity, including mask
processing, worklist construction, and other non-convolution operators.
DynConv lies at the upper end despite its low required-work ratio because every
gated block still runs learned mask-prediction kernels and exposes exceptionally
short worklists, as further discussed in
\S\ref{sec:eval:microbenchmarks}.

\paragraph{Energy.}
\sysname's latency gains also reduce per-frame board energy on both Jetson
platforms. It is the lowest-energy path for every workload
(Table~\ref{tab:e2e-energy}), reducing energy by
17.0--39.8\% relative to the lowest-energy competing path in each pair and by
28.5--72.6\% relative to dense execution.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
\sysname's latency and energy gains do not alter the numerical output of the
selected sparsity policies. For FireFlowNet and DynConv, its AEE and PCKh match the
other sparse paths at the reported precision. In the
YOLOv8 models, small floating-point differences accumulate across frames under
temporal execution; even so, \sysname's pseudo-label AP differs from the other sparse paths
by at most 0.5\% in relative terms (Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-accuracy}), confirming
output agreement across execution backends. Compared
with dense execution, the upstream sparsity policies lower AP and PCKh by only
1.6--3.5\%, while lowering FireFlowNet's AEE by 0.8\%.

\paragraph{Effective throughput.}
Under the same execution settings as the end-to-end evaluation, we estimate
full-model throughput $T$ and effective throughput $\eta T$ on RTX~3080
(Figure~\ref{fig:effective-throughput}). Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency}
reports \sysname's $\eta$ decomposition and worklist-length distribution.
\sysname sustains 35.3--89.7\% of dense throughput, the highest among sparse
paths in every workload at 1.10--1.99$\times$ that of the next-highest sparse
path.
DeltaCNN's lower raw throughput reflects the impact of routing very sparse
tiles through its fused selective path~\cite{deltacnn}. The two YOLO workloads
lie at the upper end
because their higher active ratios expose more parallelism and amortize fixed
costs.

\sysname's useful-compute ratio is 98.9\%,
96.2\%, and 95.2\% for FireFlowNet, YOLOv8n, and YOLOv8m.
DynConv is lower at 81.7\%; its low active ratio and small spatial grids yield
exceptionally short worklists, with P10, P50, and P90 lengths of 3, 43, and 389.
Padding in the issued position sequence $\mathcal{C}$ is therefore difficult
to avoid. DynConv's layer-specific gates also provide no shared activity signal
across layers, and the evaluated MPII~\cite{mpii} samples are not temporally
related and thus cannot support lagged prediction; yet its useful-compute ratio
remains well above tile skipping's 48.9\%.
Combining throughput and useful-compute ratio, \sysname achieves the
highest effective throughput in every workload, reaching 1.51--1.86$\times$
that of the strongest evaluated competing path.

Table~\ref{tab:worklist-efficiency} localizes the remaining wasted work:
despite cross-operator reuse, $\eta_{\mathrm{construct}}$ remains at
98.5--100.0\%. The selected decompositions give $\eta_{\mathrm{compute}}$
values of 99.1\%, 97.7\%, and 96.5\% for FireFlowNet, YOLOv8n, and YOLOv8m;
because DynConv has $\eta_{\mathrm{construct}}=100.0\%$, its
lower $\eta$ comes entirely from compute-stage padding of its short worklists.

\paragraph{Latency cost decomposition.}
We decompose YOLOv8n latency on RTX~3080 by execution stage
(Table~\ref{tab:cost-decomposition}), where frames from MOT16~\cite{mot16}
are classified into bins of low or high active ratios. \sysname's construction
time remains at 0.105--0.106\,ms while convolution grows from 1.09 to
1.29\,ms across the two bins; construction's latency share consequently falls
from 7.1\% to 6.2\%. Gather-scatter
shows why exact selection alone is insufficient: it has the lowest convolution
time at 0.73--0.83\,ms, but extraction, setup, and writeback put
10.9--11.0\,ms (92.2--93.1\% of total latency) in \emph{Other}. \sysname
instead spends 0.160--0.165\,ms (9.8--10.9\% of latency) on \emph{Other}, with
convolution dominant at 73.8--76.3\%. \emph{Other} aggregates residual
non-convolution work not assigned to construction, convolution, or elementwise
operators. For gather-scatter, this category can include active-coordinate
extraction, receptive-field materialization, GEMM setup, scatter/writeback, and
associated bookkeeping. Relative to Dense's 0.10\,ms in the same category,
\sysname adds only 0.06--0.07\,ms outside construction, whereas gather-scatter
adds 10.8--10.9\,ms. This contrast shows that the large residual cost comes
from the gather-scatter execution path rather than convolution arithmetic,
while direct worklist consumption avoids that data-movement bottleneck.

Each row in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} disables one mechanism.
Per-coordinate atomics (Atomic append) raise construction to 0.182\,ms through
finer serialization; convolution also rises to 1.184\,ms because the resulting
worklist loses tile-local contiguity.
A full-grid prefix-scan (Global order) raises construction to 0.246\,ms;
convolution drops marginally to 1.163\,ms, but the 0.141\,ms construction
overhead far exceeds the 0.011\,ms compute benefit, validating tile-local
segments as a sufficient approximation.
Disabling cross-operator reuse (No reuse) nearly doubles construction to
0.205\,ms while convolution is unchanged, confirming that reuse eliminates
redundant construction cost.
Fixing per-layer decomposition offline (Static level) raises convolution by
4.9\%: frames whose worklist length deviates from the calibration point
receive a mismatched configuration.
The Ideal level oracle reduces total latency by only 2.5\% below Ours,
bounding the cost of lagged observation and confirming previous-frame activity
as a sufficient proxy for level selection.

\paragraph{Stream-level batching.}
Table~\ref{tab:batch-cost-decomposition} shows how stream-level batching
changes per-frame costs. At batch size~8, gather-scatter still spends 83.0\%
of its latency in \emph{Other}, whereas tile skipping becomes the fastest
competing path at batch sizes 4 and~8. Its launch grid is determined statically
from the input shape, so increasing the batch dimension adds independent tiles
from multiple temporal streams, consistent with DeltaCNN's observation for
high-end GPUs~\cite{deltacnn}. The effect is
concentrated in convolution: from batch size~1 to~8, tile skipping's per-frame
convolution cost falls 2.68$\times$, compared with 1.63$\times$ for \sysname.
\sysname remains fastest, but its lead narrows from 1.79$\times$ to
1.49$\times$. These results, in turn, show why adaptive decomposition matters
at batch size~1: \sysname adapts its work decomposition to each short worklist
instead of relying on a larger batch to expand the launch grid.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

\sysname consumes rather than generates masks, so its gains are conditional on
the upstream policy and its activity distribution; it guarantees coverage of
requested outputs but does not improve mask quality. Across our workloads, the
evaluation shows that \sysname most effectively converts the supplied mask
sparsity into end-to-end latency reduction among the evaluated sparse paths.
The adaptive decomposition is deployment-specific and must be
recalibrated for a new model, device, or mask workload, although prediction
error affects performance rather than correctness.

We evaluate all systems in FP32, the common precision of the evaluated
masked-convolution paths, to isolate execution-model differences from
arithmetic-precision differences. Extending the worklist compute stage to
tensor cores requires a different reduction design and remains future work.
